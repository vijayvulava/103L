## Porosity and Permeability

An aquifer is a rock unit that contains extractable groundwater. A good aquifer must be both porous and permeable. Porosity is the space between grains that can hold water, expressed as the percentage of open space in the total volume of the rock. Porosity can be primary porosity or secondary porosity. Primary porosity develops at the time of sedimentation, typically created by the space between clasts. In volcanic rocks, primary porosity may result from vesicles. Secondary porosity is associated with fractures or dissolved spaces in that form after lithification. Compaction and cementation during lithification of sediments reduce porosity.  Permeability, a measure of how easily water can flow through a rock, arises from interconnected spaces that allow water to move within an aquifer. If a rock or sediment has low permeability, wells will not produce water very long before drying up. If a rock or sediment has high permeability, water will flow to the wellheads, so wells will reliably produce water for long periods.

The combination of a place to contain water (porosity) and the ability to move water (permeability) makes a good aquifer—a rock or sediment that allows the extraction of groundwater. Well-sorted sediments have higher porosity than poorly sorted sediments because there are no smaller particles filling spaces between larger particles. Shales made of clays generally have high porosity, but the pores are poorly connected, thereby giving them low permeability.

While permeability is an important measure of a porous material’s ability to transmit water, hydraulic conductivity is more commonly used by geologists to measure how easily a fluid is transmitted. Hydraulic conductivity measures both the permeability of the porous material and the properties of the water, or whatever fluid is being transmitted, like oil or gas. Because hydraulic conductivity also reflects fluid properties, such as viscosity, it is used by both petroleum geologists and hydrogeologists to describe the production capability of oil reservoirs and aquifers. High hydraulic conductivity indicates that fluid transmits rapidly through an aquifer.

Aquifers are rock layers with sufficient porosity to be a water source and enough permeability to allow wells to produce water over extended times. For rock or sediment to be considered an aquifer, its pores must be at least partially filled with water, and it must be permeable enough to transmit water. Drinking water aquifers must also contain potable water. Aquifers can vary dramatically in scale, from spanning several formations covering large regions to being a local formation in a limited area. Aquifers adequate for water supply are both permeable, porous, and potable.

## Groundwater Models

When surface water infiltrates or seeps into the ground, it generally enters the unsaturated zone, also called the vadose zone, or zone of aeration. (The only exception is if the water table is at the surface and there is no unsaturated zone.) The unsaturated zone extends from the surface to, or almost to, the water table. Pore spaces in the unsaturated zone are not completely filled with water. The saturated zone, or phreatic zone, is below the water table. In the saturated zone, the pores are completely filled with water, and the water within them is at or above atmospheric pressure. The thin capillary fringe, just below the unsaturated zone and above the water table, is a zone where the pores are completely filled with water (saturated), but the water pressure is less than atmospheric pressure.

A confining layer is a low-permeability layer above and/or below an aquifer that restricts the water from moving in and out of the aquifer. Confining layers include aquicludes, which are so impermeable that no water travels through them, and aquitards, which significantly decrease the speed at which water travels through them. Water is found in the pore spaces of saturated aquifers.

The simplest case is when an aquifer is unconfined, meaning it has no impermeable confining layer above it. Wells are conduits that extend into the ground and connect to aquifers. Wells producing water from an unconfined aquifer must be pumped to produce water. So, they must be deep enough to, at least, reach the water table. Besides providing water sources, wells are generally the means by which geologists and hydrologists measure the depth to groundwater from the land surface. Wells are also sometimes used to measure, and sometimes to add water to, an aquifer.

Confining layers above an aquifer can lead to pressurization. If the aquifer recharge area is at a higher elevation than a well, water pressure will build up beneath the confining layer (see the figure below). In some cases, the pressure will be sufficient to push water up a well. The potentiometric surface (dashed line in the figure below) represents the height that water would rise in a well penetrating the pressurized aquifer system. If the potentiometric surface is higher than the land elevation, connections from the pressurized aquifer to the surface, such as faults or wells, can cause springs or flowing wells (artesian wells) to form at the surface. This, in effect, is what happens when you lift up the ends of a garden hose that contains water. If you lift one end high enough, water will flow from the other end even though there is a low spot in the middle.

## Karst

Karst refers to landscapes and hydrologic features created by the dissolution of limestone. Surface water enters a karst system through sinkholes, losing streams, and disappearing streams. Changes in base level can cause rivers flowing over limestone to dissolve it and sink into the ground. As water continues to dissolve limestone, it can leave behind intricate networks of caves and narrow passages. Often, further dissolution will follow, expanding fractures in the limestone. Water exits the karst system as springs and rises. In mountainous terrane, dissolution can extend through the entire vertical profile of the mountain, with caverns dropping thousands of feet.

Karst can be found anywhere there is limestone and other soluble subterranean substances, such as salt deposits. Mostly, however, when we think of karst, we think of features like sinkholes, caverns, disappearing streams, and towers that form in limestone terranes. CO2 in the atmosphere dissolves readily in water vapor, forming carbonic acid. So, most of the water droplets that form clouds and rain or snow are slightly acidic. Karst forms when carbonic acid dissolves calcite (calcium carbonate) in limestone.
